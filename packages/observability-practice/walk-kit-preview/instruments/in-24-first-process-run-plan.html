    Run your team's first process¶

    One facilitated working session — about 90 minutes — turns a business process you pick into your first service profile: what the process owes, to whom, and the signals that show whether it's holding. This page is the run sheet. Run it once with facilitation; after that your team runs its own.

    New to the idea? Read the why first.

    Why business observability explains why observability starts from business health, not telemetry. This page is the how — it assumes you're sold on the idea and ready to do it.

    Before the session¶

    Get four things ready. None needs tooling; two go faster with it.

    1 — Pick one business process. One the business notices when it fails, and one the team knows well. A good first pick beats a perfect one.

    2 — Get three people in the room (however your org names the roles):

    • Business — knows what the process owes and to whom (product owner, BA, or the ops lead who fields the complaints).
    • Engineering — knows the applications inside the process.
    • Monitoring — owns today's alerts for those applications.

    3 — Build the process inventory. One table: the process → every application that participates, in order → where each runs. Pull it from architecture docs and runbooks; if none exist, write it from the engineers' heads. Most teams find this table exists nowhere — producing it is your first real output, not prep overhead.

    4 — Get the code-level picture in front of the engineers. Run DeepWiki (a tool that reads a repo and builds a readable wiki of how the code works) against the applications' repos for flow diagrams and service descriptions, then have the engineers correct it — the documented flow must match the real one before anything is measured against it. No tooling yet? A hand-drawn flow the engineers agree on works for process one.

    Then read the worked example — it shows what "done" looks like, so the room can check its own output against it.

    No room yet? Start solo.

    Draft the stakeholder chains yourself from documents and your own knowledge, in the session order below — and mark every expectation and signal NEEDS VALIDATION. A solo pass is a draft, not a finished profile. The session then runs as confirm-and-correct — faster than starting blank, and nothing self-answered slips through as settled.

    The session — 90 minutes¶

    One person facilitates and asks; the room answers. Run the segments in this order — it follows how business people actually reason, so traceability builds as you go instead of being reconnected afterward.

    # Segment Time You walk out with
    1 Context 10 min the inventory table, confirmed in the room
    2 Stakeholder chains — the core 50 min one closed chain per stakeholder
    3 Impact 10 min a cost attached to each expectation
    4 Technical signals 15 min existing monitors connected upward
    5 Close 5 min open questions, each with an owner and a date

    1 · Context — 10 min¶

    What is this process, what does it participate in, who depends on it. Confirm the inventory table in the room.

    2 · Stakeholder chains — 50 min · the core¶

    One stakeholder at a time, complete before moving on:

    • Who are they? A named role — the operations team, the downstream reconciliation group, the customer.
    • What do they expect of this process, as a measurable sentence? ("Files arrive by 6am." "A transaction completes end to end in 10 seconds.")
    • What signal would tell us each expectation is met or missed? Name it, even if nothing measures it today.
    • Close the chain before the next stakeholder: does every expectation have a signal? Gaps stay on the page as gaps.

    3 · Impact — 10 min¶

    When each expectation is missed, what does it cost — money, customers, regulatory exposure, downstream teams blocked? Attach the cost to the expectation it belongs to.

    4 · Technical signals — 15 min¶

    Now bring in the engineers: for each business signal, which application-level signals would explain a breach (workflow errors, queue depth, latency A→B)? Which system-level signals sit under those? This is where years of bottom-up monitoring get connected upward instead of thrown away.

    5 · Close — 5 min¶

    Read back every question the room couldn't answer — each with an owner and a date.

    The session's output

    A filled service profile: stakeholders → expectations → business signals → supporting application and system signals, plus impact notes and the gap list.

    After the session¶

    Set measurements and targets. For each signal: which source answers it — logs, APM, synthetics — and what the target is. Where no target was ever agreed, derive a starting range from what "normal" has looked like over recent months, alert on deviation, and replace it with a real agreement when one exists.

    Wire the response. Every signal that can breach gets an owner and a next action. From here it's the reliability practice you already run — now anchored to the process map instead of tribal memory.

    Let the profile drive the artifacts. The filled profile is the source dashboards, alerts, and playbooks generate from. Until that machinery is local: keep the profile on a wiki page, build the dashboard once by hand from it, and map question → signal → tool.

    Process two and three¶

    Run the next process with less facilitation — the people from session one can now lead their own. Log friction as it happens (what confused the room, what blocked progress — 30 seconds an entry) and fix the next run from the log. A signal that traces to no expectation is a cleanup candidate, not a keeper.
