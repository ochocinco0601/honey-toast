# Purpose: orient someone to a course

**Fits when** the subject is a whole body of material the viewer works through in order
(a training, a course, a tutorial series), and what the viewer needs first is not any one skill in
it but to know what it will let them do, whether it is for them, how it is laid out, and where to
start. Material used for reference rather than worked through in order (a documentation set, an
API reference) has no learner order and needs its own purpose.

Its content comes from the established practice for opening a course: Quality Matters Standard 1
(course overview and introduction: learners can find how to get started and where the parts are,
and the purpose and structure are stated), Gagné's first three events of instruction (gain
attention, state what the learner will be able to do, connect to what they already know),
Keller's ARCS model (of its four conditions, relevance and confidence matter most here: the
viewer sees why it matters to their work and that they can do what it asks), and Ausubel's advance
organizer (a short frame given before the material, at a higher level than the material, that the
parts then hang on). Adult-learning practice (Knowles) adds that an adult learner needs to know why
before they will start, and arrives with experience that shapes what they expect.

## What to work out about the viewer before the brief

- **Why they arrive and what they expect.** Did they choose this, or is it required? What might
  they expect it to be? Work it out from the material and its project records, never as a
  question to the person (Knowles: their prior experience shapes what they expect). Write it as
  the brief's Problem line, marked as your assumption so the person can correct it. If the
  records say nothing about it, say so rather than supplying an expectation.
- **Which of them the material serves, and by which path.** If it has more than one kind of
  learner or more than one route, name them.

## The lines this purpose adds to the brief

- **Problem.** What the viewer assumes or does not know on arrival that would stop them starting,
  or start them on the wrong part.
- **Action.** Which part they open first, and why that one: a verb they perform, such as "opens
  the first part, because the others build on it". Never "understands the course". Where the part
  is and what it needs belong to the material's own pages, not to this line.
- **Outcome.** What the whole material will let them do in their own work, in their terms, stated
  so they can judge whether it is for them.
- **Organizer.** The one idea the parts hang on, at a higher level than any part, and what it
  connects to that the viewer already knows (Ausubel; Gagné's third event). Not a list of parts:
  an outline is not an organizer.
- **Map.** The parts, in the order a learner takes them, each with what it lets them do and who it
  is for, placed on the organizer. Only the parts' purpose: what each teaches belongs to the part.
- **Format.** How the material runs (self-paced or led, hands-on or watched), from the source.
  Never how long a part takes or how many parts there are: those change as the material is
  edited, and the material's own pages carry them (see "State nothing the subject will change" in
  SKILL.md).
- **Entry.** What the viewer must have before the first part (a tool installed, an account) and
  what they must already know or be able to do (QM 1.6, 1.7), and where the material covers each.
  The piece relies on that place; it does not restate it.

## Whether an explainer is the fix, for a course

First ask whether the material's own opening (its home page or introduction) already does the job.
If it states what the material lets you do, who it is for and where to start, a piece that says
the same again is not the fix. A piece earns its place only by what text cannot do there:
showing the thing at work, for a viewer who will not read the page. Say which in the Fix line.

An overview fixes not knowing what the material is, whether it is for them, and where to start.
It does not fix not having what the material needs, such as a tool or an account (the material's
owner), and it does not fix a reason not to take it, such as no time given or nobody asking for it
(whoever requires the course). Name both in the Fix line when the material's records show them.

**The job aid** for a course is usually the material's own home page, which already holds the map.
Do not make a second one. Make one only when no page in the material lists the parts in order with
how to start; then it is one page in this order: what the material lets you do, before you start,
the parts in order (each with one line), and where to begin.

## The first moment, for a course

Hand the walk these with the brief. The viewer has the material's home page, or a link to it, and
may have nothing the material needs open, installed or signed in. Their first act after the piece
is to open the first part. How they find the piece and that part from where they are is part of the
walk, and so is every installation, account or permission the part needs: the walk checks that the
material's own pages say where each comes from. A gap there goes to the material's owner, never
into the script.

## Rules for the script

- The opening (SKILL.md, "Every piece opens before it explains") names the material, says this is
  an overview of it, and says what the viewer will be able to do in their own work once they have
  taken it. Never a list of what the piece will cover ("what they let you do, how they fit, and
  where to begin"), which tells the viewer nothing.
- Then show what the material will let them do in their own work, concretely, before any part is
  introduced (Gagné's second event; ARCS relevance). Concrete, for an overview, means the subject
  at work and what the viewer can then do; it never means a walk through the material's
  exercises or practice tasks, which the parts teach. "Concretely" read the other way pulls the
  plan into replaying one part's practice task.
- Then show the organizer as a frame on its own, with no parts listed on it (Ausubel; Gagné's
  third event). Each part is placed on it as the walk reaches it, which keeps SKILL.md's "Never
  preview a list you are about to walk" intact.
- Walk the parts once, in the order a learner takes them, each in one scene at most, grouping
  parts by their place on the organizer when there are more parts than scenes. Name each part by
  its title and say what it lets the viewer do: the title is what they will meet next (SKILL.md,
  "State nothing the subject will change"). Never teach a part's content.
- If the Problem line names an expectation the records support, show what makes the material
  different from it rather than asserting it.
- The last scene is the first act: which part comes first, by its title, and why. Where it lives,
  what it needs and its first step are on the material's own pages, which change with hosting and
  editing; the piece leaves them there, or it goes stale the first time the material moves.

## The reviewer's outcome question

**Could someone who watched this say what the material will let them do, whether it is for them,
and how its parts fit together, and say which part to open first and why, without asking
anyone?** Walk it as the viewer: after the last scene, which part do they open first, and where do
they hesitate or go to the wrong part? When they reach a later part, could they place it on the
organizer?
